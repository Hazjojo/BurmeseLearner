# Retrieval and tone training beat gamified bloat

> **Scope note (added after review):** this app is for a single, non-commercial learner. The sections on engagement, retention and monetisation (streaks, daily-user growth, Duolingo's business data, licensing for redistribution) are context only. They describe what keeps users in commercial apps, not what makes people learn. [`ROADMAP.md`](../ROADMAP.md) §2 separates the learning evidence from the engagement evidence and is the source of truth for decisions.

The evidence says a language app works when it makes learners **retrieve spaced, audio-backed material and produce it**. Game mechanics do not move proficiency. Practice testing and distributed practice are the only two study techniques with strong support across meta-analyses, and every independent and vendor study of named apps finds the same thing: **time on task predicts gains**. Streaks, leagues and gems mainly move engagement metrics. For Burmese the lean core is spaced recall of high-frequency colloquial words and chunks, with native audio on every item. Add a multi-speaker tone and phonation perception trainer, one-screen grammar notes on particles, a script trainer that is Unicode-correct and safe against Zawgyi, and graded micro-stories built almost entirely from words the learner already knows. That combination fills a real market gap: Duolingo has no Burmese course, and no existing app is known to train Burmese tones systematically. The phase-2 speaking goal is feasible only in a narrower form than the vision implies. Burmese speech-to-text and text-to-speech now exist as cloud services. But no pronunciation-assessment API supports Burmese, Burmese tones are cued by phonation and duration as well as pitch, real-time speech-to-speech in Burmese is unconfirmed, and no frontier LLM clears 50 on the SEA-HELM Burmese benchmark. Phase 2 should therefore be a turn-based pipeline (speech-to-text, then LLM, then text-to-speech) running constrained role-plays, with coarse "hear, say, compare" feedback, and it should not try to act as an automated pronunciation judge. Firebase can host the whole phase-1 app for roughly $0–2 a month at hobby scale. The real cost and risk sit in content: native recordings, a curated colloquial word list and licensing.

## Two techniques carry most of the learning evidence

The strongest finding in learning science is also the cheapest to build. In Rowland's meta-analysis, **practice testing beats restudy at Hedges' g = 0.50, rising to g = 0.69 when retention is measured more than a day later** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/)). Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten common study techniques and rated **only two "high utility": practice testing and distributed practice** ([PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/26173288/)). In the same review, rereading, highlighting and the keyword mnemonic came out low utility. For second languages, Kim and Webb pooled **98 effect sizes from 48 experiments (N = 3,411)**. Spacing gave a medium-to-large benefit. Short gaps matched long gaps on immediate tests but fell behind on delayed tests, and **expanding schedules performed no better than equal-interval schedules** ([BL record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100152978253_0x000001)). Cepeda's study of more than 1,350 people adds a design rule: the best gap grows with how long you want to remember. It falls from **about 20–40% of the retention interval for a one-week test to about 5–10% for a one-year test** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)).

These results reframe the choice of scheduler. If expanding and fixed intervals teach about equally well, the scheduler's job is efficiency: fewer wasted reviews at a target retention. That is where FSRS claims its advantage. The supporting benchmark is open data and code, but the FSRS developers run it themselves. Figures such as "FSRS-5 beats trainable SM-2 in 97.4% of collections" circulate on forums and were not checked against the official table ([AnkiWeb forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/fsrs-vs-sm2-in-2-seperate-profiles/52171)). No peer-reviewed trial compares *learning outcomes* under FSRS and SM-2. The case for FSRS is still easy to make because it costs almost nothing. **ts-fsrs 6.0.0 (MIT, released 28 October 2026) defaults to FSRS-7** and has an immutable `scheduler.review()` call that returns a new card plus a review log ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/ts-fsrs); [README](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/ts-fsrs)). That fits an offline-first browser client well. Pin the model version explicitly. Treat a desired-retention setting near 0.9 as a sensible default, not as a tested optimum.

The *format* of retrieval matters as much as the schedule. Nakata separates receptive recall, productive recall and two recognition formats. **Productive recall (English to Burmese) especially builds spelling and form knowledge**, while recognition formats helped the first form–meaning link ([IRAL 2015](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)). Secondary summaries of Nakata and Webb add a trade-off: receptive recall yields more words per minute, and productive recall gives the biggest gains on productive tests ([Serfaty thesis](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/196300/1/JONATHAN%20SERFATY_PhD_THESIS.pdf)). So multiple choice belongs only at first exposure. The main review loop should be recall: hear or see Burmese, produce the meaning, then grade it. A productive stage, where the learner says or assembles the Burmese, should unlock once a card has been learned receptively.

Three moderately supported additions complete the memory core. First, **explicit grammar instruction beats implicit instruction** (Norris and Ortega: d ≈ 1.13 vs 0.54). There is an important caveat: **only about 16% of the underlying studies measured free production**, so part of the advantage may come from test format ([Columbia SALT](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1434)). Short, focused explanations are justified; long grammar lectures are not. Second, a meta-analysis of 17 studies found a **large effect for collocation instruction (d = 1.415)**. The studies were mostly small ([IRAL](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2021-0218/html)). That supports teaching frequent chunks as units. Burmese grammar lives largely in particles, so chunk cards carry grammar implicitly. Third, for corrective feedback, **prompts that make the learner self-correct outperformed recasts that simply supply the answer**, in a meta-analysis of 15 classroom studies (N = 827) ([Lyster & Saito](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)). Applying that to an app is an extrapolation from classrooms, but a cheap one: when an answer is wrong, highlight the faulty syllable or say "check the tone", and reveal the answer only after a second attempt.

Input matters, but the strong "input alone suffices" claim gets no meta-analytic support in this research. Extensive reading shows adult effects of d ≈ 0.70 ([NFLRC](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/354)). A propensity-score reanalysis argues that selection bias inflates this and estimates about **0.37** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188915)). The firmer finding is about coverage. Unassisted comprehension needs **about 95% of running words known as a minimum and 98% as the optimum**. In English that means roughly 8,000 word families for optimal reading ([Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/66648/1/22_1_10125_66648_laufer.pdf)). A beginner in a distant language with a new script and no shared cognates will not reach 95% of authentic Burmese for a long time. Pure comprehensible-input platforms that recommend around 600 hours of listening before speaking ([FluentU](https://www.fluentu.com/blog/reviews/dreaming-spanish/)) are therefore a poor fit. The workable version is deliberate SRS on high-frequency items plus narrow, authored input in which nearly every word is already known.

The evidence also identifies what to leave out. Pashler's review found **virtually no support for matching instruction to "learning styles"** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104/)). The keyword mnemonic helps immediate recall, but later forgetting can be faster than with rote rehearsal unless pictures are provided ([UCF 1992](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus1990/1045); [UCF 1996](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib1990/601)). Burmese also has few English sound-alikes, which makes keyword mnemonics an even weaker fit.

## Commercial apps prove engagement, not proficiency

The outcome evidence for named apps is thinner than their marketing suggests. Almost all of it is vendor-funded or vendor-co-authored, and it measures receptive skills far more than speaking. Duolingo's peer-reviewed study is co-authored by staff. It found that learners who finished beginning Spanish or French reached **ACTFL Intermediate Low in reading but only Novice High in listening** ([NAU](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/evaluating-the-reading-and-listening-outcomes-of-beginning-level-/)). The press release then upgraded that to "Intermediate" ([Duolingo IR](https://investors.duolingo.com/news-releases/news-release-details/leading-language-research-journal-publishes-study-showing)). The one independent Duolingo case study had **nine participants**. Gains correlated moderately with time spent, and learners reported "frustration with instructional materials" ([ReCALL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/abs/mobileassisted-language-learning-a-duolingo-case-study/A4D7C8F71782A37D258C19F357DDBCBE)). Babbel's 12-week study, co-authored by an employee, found **about 59% of completers rose at least one ACTFL sublevel in speaking**, and **study time was the strongest predictor on every measure** ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/06/200609095027.htm)). The widely quoted "hours per college semester" figures come from commissioned designs, mostly by the same two researchers: Babbel 15–21 hours, Busuu 22, Duolingo 34, Rosetta Stone 45–60. They are measured against a weak baseline and do not compare with one another ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/01/17/study-learning-spanish-with-duolingo-can-be-more-effective-than-college-classes-or-rosetta-stone/amp); [Babbel](https://press.babbel.com/en/releases/2016-09-29-Efficacy_Study.html)). The defensible reading is that beginner apps get learners to about A1–A2. Speaking is the least-served skill, and no app has been shown to produce advanced proficiency on its own.

Duolingo's own disclosures show that engagement machinery works on engagement metrics. In Q3 2026, **DAU growth reaccelerated to 29% year on year**. Management said "the vast majority of the improvement in active users came from retention," driven by "hundreds of experiments... like notifications and streaks" ([Finviz](https://finviz.com/news/414652/duolingo-reports-third-quarter-2026-results)). Its blog reports that learners who reach a 7-day streak are **2.4× more likely to return the next day**. Separating the daily goal from the streak raised Day-14 retention by 3.3% ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit); [Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). Those numbers are confounded by selection, since motivated users build streaks, and no source links streaks to proficiency. Independent commentary describes "streak creep": users come to value extending the streak over the activity itself, quit entirely after breaking it, and do the shortest possible lesson to protect it ([The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)). Systematic reviews of gamification in language learning find positive associations but **few studies that isolate a causal effect** ([Luo 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10135444/)).

The backlash record is just as instructive for a lean product. Duolingo replaced Hearts with Energy, which drains on correct answers too for free users. It drew multiple Change.org petitions ([Fandom wiki](https://duolingo.fandom.com/wiki/Energy); [Change.org](https://www.change.org/p/duolingo-hearts-not-energy)). Its April 2025 "AI-first" memo coincided with **third-party estimates of DAU growth falling from 56% in February to 37% in June 2025**, and the stock ended 2025 **down 46%** ([Slashdot/Motley Fool](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/25/06/28/2036249/); [AOL/Motley Fool](https://www.aol.com/articles/why-duolingo-stock-lost-46-203300015.html)). Two lessons follow: never meter practice, and treat perceived low-quality AI content as a retention risk. That second lesson is sharpest with the serious-learner segment a Burmese app would attract.

What sustains self-study is better explained by motivation research than by game mechanics. In a 2026 study of 305 app users, **more autonomous motivation predicted shorter gaps between sessions and a longer overall usage span** ([LLT](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1273/)). A survival analysis of 3,670 adult mobile learners found that regular use with short breaks predicted fewer drop-outs ([SSLA 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C1186B329F808A11DDF2C748E77FD1EE/S0272263124000354a.pdf/div-class-title-acceptance-and-engagement-patterns-of-mobile-assisted-language-learning-among-non-conventional-adult-l2-learners-a-survival-analysis-div.pdf)). MOOC drop-out is **heaviest in the first days and levels off after about two weeks** ([AERA Open](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858416657002)). Retention design should therefore go into the first fortnight: a fast first win, a goal the learner chooses, and visible competence ("you can now read these syllables"). A gentle streak tied to clearing due reviews, with freezes, adds habit support without punishment. Experienced self-learners already assemble a minimal toolkit: a structured audio foundation, an SRS for words that keep slipping, graded input, and eventually human conversation. Each tool is seen as partial ([GummySearch aggregator](https://gummysearch.com/tools/best-products/language-learning-app)). A lean Burmese app can combine the first three and hand off the fourth, at least until phase 2.

## Burmese breaks pitch-only tone tools and English-based assumptions

Burmese tone is not "just pitch," and that one fact reshapes both the perception trainer and the phase-2 feedback design. The four-way contrast (low, high, creaky, checked) is carried by **pitch, phonation, duration and intensity together**. Watkins notes that "phonation type is thus only partially responsible for tonal contrasts" ([Watkins 1997](https://core.ac.uk/download/2783471.pdf)). The acoustic literature itself disagrees on the details. One study separates creaky and stopped tones mainly by vowel quality ([UT Austin](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/19915)). Another argues that F0 is more primary than phonation ([Lai](https://icu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/5250/files/ICUWPL18-11_Lai.pdf)). A third found **no increased creakiness in creaky-tone syllables in sentence context**, where F0 height did the distinguishing ([Lee, Perkins & Villegas](https://www2.ninjal.ac.jp/past-projects/phonology/ICPP2015-Lee%26Perkins%26Villegas.pdf)). These cues work differently from Mandarin's. Native Burmese listeners discriminated Mandarin tone pairs *less* accurately than English listeners with no tone background, which the authors partly attribute to the phonation cues in Burmese ([Tsukada & Kondo 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30343621)). The research found **no published study of English speakers perceiving or producing Burmese tones**. Every tone recommendation here is extrapolated from Mandarin, Thai and general phonetic-training research.

That extrapolation still favours perception training. In the classic study, eight sessions of high-variability phonetic training (HVPT) raised American learners' Mandarin tone identification **from about 69% to 90%**. The gain generalized to new talkers and held at six months ([Wang et al. 1999](https://kuppl.ku.edu/sites/kuppl/files/documents/publications/wang_etal_revision.pdf)). A meta-analysis of 31 studies found that perceptual training **transfers to production with small-to-medium effects** ([Applied Psycholinguistics 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38D8F5CE65DC708137B0E95F97C6BC7)). A 2025 phonetic-training meta-analysis reports d = 0.762, with perceptual training stronger than production training ([JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/2024_JSLHR-24-00432)). One caveat matters for budgeting: the "many talkers" ingredient itself is contested. A talker-variability meta-analysis found g = 0.46, which **dropped to g = −0.04 after outlier removal and publication-bias correction** ([JSLHR 2021](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_JSLHR-21-00181)). Recording four to six native speakers is still worthwhile. It costs little extra once a recording session is booked, and it cannot hurt generalization. The evidence for pitch-contour visualization is mixed: one small study found no production benefit ([OpenBU](https://open.bu.edu/items/abd739eb-7dd7-420d-bb61-48d7e7e8e003)), while contours paired with explicit shape information beat tone numbers for identification ([Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1410795/xml/nlm)). For Burmese, a pitch-only display would also miss duration and phonation. Any visual should show length as well as contour, and should be framed as "compare with the native speaker," not as a verdict.

The script and its encoding make up the second Burmese-specific problem, and an English-language developer is likely to underestimate it. Myanmar officially switched to Unicode on **"U-Day," 1 October 2019** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zawgyi_font)). Yet a 2024 industry article still called the legacy Zawgyi encoding "the most widely used font in Myanmar" ([GALA](https://gala-global.org/knowledge-center/professional-development/articles/zawgyi-vs-unicode-burmese-font-dilemma)), and no 2025–2026 figure was found. Any text a learner pastes from Facebook or Viber may therefore be Zawgyi: it looks garbled, or looks right but compares wrong. Google's **myanmar-tools** detects and converts Zawgyi in JavaScript and Python ([GitHub](https://github.com/google/myanmar-tools/)). Its npm package was last published in 2022, and registries disagree on its licence, listing Apache-2.0 and "other" ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/myanmar-tools)). Segmentation is easier than expected. In a local test, **`Intl.Segmenter('my', {granularity: 'word'})` split a test sentence into correct words**, but **grapheme segmentation does not yield Burmese syllables**: ကျွန်တော် broke into four non-linguistic pieces ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Intl/Segmenter)). Syllable tiles and tone drills therefore need a regex syllable breaker. Noto Sans Myanmar, Noto Serif Myanmar and Padauk are all served by Google Fonts and can be bundled for offline use ([Google Fonts](https://fonts.google.com/noto/specimen/Noto+Sans+Myanmar)).

No research addressed how adults best learn a new script, so the script trainer rests on general principles. It should pair every glyph with audio, use spaced recall in both directions (audio to glyph and glyph to sound), and offer app-provided picture mnemonics, since self-generated ones did not reduce forgetting ([UCF](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib1990/601)). Productive typing or tile assembly trains orthography ([Nakata](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)). An in-app keyboard or tile picker is needed, because most learners will not have a Burmese keyboard installed. Romanization should be a fading scaffold with visible tone marks, because no single standard exists and informal spellings omit tone ([Okell publications](https://eprints.soas.ac.uk/view/people/Okell=3AJohn=3A=3A.html)).

Register and content supply complete the picture. Burmese is diglossic. The literary style dominates formal writing; the colloquial style is used in speech and informal writing, and the two differ mostly in **grammatical particles** ([core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/481747684.pdf)). Okell's literary-style course assumes the learner already knows the colloquial style, which confirms that **colloquial-first is the established teaching order** ([Cambridge LC](https://www.langcen.cam.ac.uk/resources/langb/data/bm_108.html)). That collides with the available data. The best open frequency data, the **myWord dictionaries (MIT)** built from a hand-segmented corpus of more than 500,000 sentences, skews toward written style ([GitHub](https://github.com/ye-kyaw-thu/myWord)). The best open audio, **Google's OpenSLR SLR80 (CC BY-SA 4.0)**, is read, formal speech ([OpenSLR](https://www.openslr.org/80)). FLORES+ is news-like (CC BY-SA 4.0) ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/openlanguagedata/flores_plus)). Tatoeba's audio is licensed per contributor and must be checked recording by recording ([Tatoeba](https://tatoeba.org/oc/downloads)). Okell's *Burmese by Ear*, the strongest free audio course, is free to download but carries **no open licence**, so the app should link to it rather than embed it ([SOAS](https://eprints.soas.ac.uk/18521)). No open Burmese–English learner dictionary exists either. The app must curate its own colloquial core vocabulary and commission native recordings. The upside is a near-empty field: Duolingo offers no Burmese course (by a competitor's account) ([Ling](https://ling-app.com/blog/burmese-language-app/)), and the App Store offers mostly phrasebooks and a handwriting tracer ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1425465073)).

## Phase-2 speech works as a cascade, not as a pronunciation judge

The building blocks for a speaking app now exist for Burmese, but each one is weaker than it is for major languages. On the speech-to-text side, Azure lists **my-MM for real-time, batch and fast transcription** ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/language-support)), and Google's Chirp 3 lists Burmese as **Preview** ([Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/speech-to-text/docs/models/chirp-3)). ElevenLabs claims **21.7% WER on FLEURS Burmese** for Scribe v1 and puts zero-shot Whisper large-v3 at 100% ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/speech-to-text/burmese)). Open fine-tunes report CERs of roughly **13–24% on read FLEURS speech** ([BuzzASR](https://huggingface.co/BuzzASR/burmese); [SeamlessM4T fine-tune](https://github.com/Hein-HtetSan/myanmar-asr)). One MMS fine-tune reports a CER of 6.66 on a dialogue corpus ([HF](https://huggingface.co/myandev/Meta-MMS-300M-ASR)). CER is the honest metric here: Burmese has no word spaces, so WER depends on the segmenter, as BuzzASR's 97% WER beside 24% CER shows. The critical unknown is that **no source measured accuracy on learner Burmese**. Recognizers trained on native speech also tend to "auto-correct" toward plausible words, so a learner's wrong tone may still be transcribed as the right word. That helps conversation flow but makes the recognizer useless as a tone judge.

On the output side, **Azure's my-MM-NilarNeural and my-MM-ThihaNeural** are generally available and support SSML rate control, which suits slow-then-normal playback ([Microsoft Tech Community](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/azure-ai/azure-speech-s-neural-tts-empowers-organizations-to-serve-users/ba-p/2920882)). ElevenLabs also lists Burmese ([ElevenLabs v3](https://elevenlabs.io/v3)). No naturalness or tone-accuracy evaluation exists for any Burmese voice, so TTS used for drills needs native review. The gap that matters most for the vision is scoring: **Azure Pronunciation Assessment covers 33 locales, and Burmese is not among them**, and no Burmese CAPT system was found ([GitHub mirror](https://github.com/MicrosoftDocs/azure-ai-docs/blob/main/articles/ai-services/speech-service/includes/language-support/pronunciation-assessment.md)). "Have their pronunciation judged" therefore means building a custom stack. A speech recognizer or constrained match checks that the right syllables were said. Then F0 extraction, normalized per speaker, plus a duration comparison against a native reference, shows the two curves overlaid. Phonation and aspiration scoring would stay experimental, because creak is inconsistent across speakers and contexts and noisy on phone microphones. Browser pitch tracking with **pitchy (MIT)** is viable for live display. Note that pitchfinder is GPL-3.0 ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/pitchy)).

Conversation runs into a model-quality ceiling. On SEA-HELM Burmese, the reported leader scored **49.56, and "every model on Burmese fails to clear 50"** (secondary tracker, leaderboard not checked) ([Digital in Asia](https://digitalinasia.com/llm-benchmarks-asian-languages-tour/)). The BURMESE-SAN benchmark finds "clear performance gaps between model families" ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.16/)). These are formal-register tasks, and colloquial spoken Burmese is likely weaker still. Native speech-to-speech is **not confirmed for Burmese** on either OpenAI Realtime or Gemini Live. Firebase's list of languages supported by all audio-generating models omits Burmese ([Firebase AI Logic](https://firebase.google.com/docs/ai-logic/live-api/configuration)). The dependable route is a **turn-based pipeline**: the learner speaks, Burmese STT transcribes, a text LLM replies under a scenario script with a vocabulary list and register instructions, and Azure TTS speaks. This also gives the app transcripts to display and check. A target of about two seconds to first audio and a cost of a few cents per active minute are engineering estimates, not measurements. One anchor is OpenAI's gpt-realtime-whisper at **$0.017 per minute**, against token-priced realtime audio at **$32/$64 per million input/output tokens** ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime-whisper); [OpenAI realtime](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime)).

The pedagogical evidence supports doing this, within limits. A meta-analysis of ASR-based pronunciation training found a **medium effect, g = 0.69 across 15 studies**. **Short treatments had a negligible effect (g = 0.07) and medium-duration treatments a large one (g = 1.01)** ([ReCALL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)). A secondary summary of the same paper reports that explicit feedback beat indirect feedback (g = 0.86 vs 0.50), and that **segmental gains were more than double suprasegmental ones (g = 0.82 vs 0.37)** ([Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/116596524/The_effectiveness_of_automatic_speech_recognition_in_ESL_EFL_pronunciation_A_meta_analysis)). Tones are suprasegmental, which is the weaker category for recognizer-only feedback, so dedicated visual comparison beats a pass/fail transcript. Speech-enabled LLM chatbots now have a 19-study meta-analysis covering learning and anxiety outcomes ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/415402675_Effects_of_speech-enabled_LLM-based_chatbots_on_L2_learning_outcomes_and_affective_domains_a_multilevel_meta-analysis)). In one controlled study, **78% of chatbot users reported lower anxiety** ([Nature HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05550-z)). A Replika study, though, found that difficulty being understood may *raise* anxiety ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391455139_A_comparison_of_the_effects_of_AI-based_chatbots_and_peer_interactions_on_speaking_anxiety_among_EFL_learners)). With Burmese recognizers erring on 13–25% of characters, that failure mode is the central design problem. The app should check content before showing any score ("I didn't catch that," never a low score), show what it heard, and allow retry or typed fallback. Scores should stay coarse, such as "pitch shape: good / try higher / too long," rather than a falsely precise 0–100.

## Firebase runs the core app for almost nothing

The phase-1 architecture is a static, offline-first progressive web app, and it fits Firebase's free allowances. Course content (notes, lessons, card templates) lives as **versioned JSON in git**. It is built at deploy time and precached, which costs no Firestore reads and makes the content diffable. Firestore holds only per-user state: settings, FSRS card state keyed by `noteId__cardType`, and batched review logs that keep enough fields to run the optimizer later. The arithmetic is favourable. Standard-edition Firestore gives **50,000 reads and 20,000 writes per day free**, then charges **$0.09 per 100,000 writes** ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/firestore/pricing)). A hundred learners doing 100 reviews a day with unbatched logs would hit exactly the daily free write quota. Batching logs per session roughly halves that, and even an overage costs well under a dollar a month. Firestore's persistent local cache is now configured through `persistentLocalCache`; the older `enableIndexedDbPersistence` is deprecated ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/firestore/manage-data/enable-offline)). Anonymous sign-in on first launch, later linked to Google or email, needs merge logic for the `auth/credential-already-in-use` case ([Firebase reference](https://firebase.google.com/docs/reference/js/v8/firebase.User)).

Three platform facts shape the phasing. First, **Cloud Storage has required the Blaze plan since 3 February 2026** ([Firebase FAQ](https://firebase.google.com/docs/storage/faqs-storage-changes-announced-sept-2024)). The leanest phase-1 option is therefore to ship normalized curriculum audio as hashed static files on Hosting. At roughly 20 KB per clip, 2,000 clips come to about 40 MB. Storage can wait until learner recordings need server-side processing. Second, offline audio needs deliberate Workbox configuration: **runtime caching of streamed media fails because playback fetches partial 206 responses**, so lessons must be precached or added explicitly, with `RangeRequestsPlugin` ([Chrome Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/workbox/serving-cached-audio-and-video)). AAC in .m4a is the safe single format across iOS versions. Third, phase-2 AI calls need App Check and server-held secrets. Firebase AI Logic can call Gemini from the client, and its Developer API free tier works on the free Spark plan. But **App Check enforcement became mandatory for AI Logic on 2 November 2026** ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/ai-logic/migrate-from-preview)), and reCAPTCHA Enterprise is free only up to 10,000 assessments a month ([App Check](https://firebase.google.com/docs/app-check)). Third-party Burmese STT and TTS vendors require a 2nd-gen callable function with `defineSecret` and `enforceAppCheck`, which means Blaze ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/functions/config-env)). One trap: running the FSRS optimizer in the browser via WASI needs COOP/COEP headers, which can break Google sign-in popups. Per-user optimization should therefore be a rare, server-side or offline job, if it happens at all ([npm binding](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@open-spaced-repetition/binding)). Recording works across browsers if the app probes `audio/webm;codecs=opus` first and falls back to `audio/mp4` for Safari before 18.4 ([Media Codings](https://media-codings.com/articles/recording-cross-browser-compatible-media)).

## Conclusion: what to build, defer and skip

The evidence turns the usual app-building instinct around. Effort should go into **content and audio quality**, not features, because the techniques that work (spaced recall, perceptual tone training, brief explicit notes, high-coverage input) are cheap to code and expensive to fill well. For Burmese the scarce asset is a native-voiced, colloquial, frequency-ordered core of a few thousand items with multi-speaker tone minimal pairs. Whoever builds that owns an empty category. The speaking vision should be reframed from "the app judges your pronunciation" to "the app helps you hear the difference and lets you rehearse safely." That is what current Burmese speech technology and the CAPT evidence actually support. Learning should also be measured directly, with periodic listening and recall checks, because streak and DAU numbers will look healthy whether or not learners improve.

### Key risks

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Content bottleneck** | No open colloquial frequency list, learner dictionary or colloquial audio exists; myWord and SLR80 skew written and formal ([myWord](https://github.com/ye-kyaw-thu/myWord); [OpenSLR](https://www.openslr.org/80)) | Budget for native-speaker curation and recording before code; start with about 500 items and tone sets |
| **Evidence transfer** | No study of English speakers learning Burmese tones or script exists; all tone guidance comes from Mandarin and Thai ([Tsukada & Kondo](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30343621)) | Instrument the app with pre/post perception tests and treat early users as a pilot |
| **Speech feedback misfires** | Recognizer accuracy on learner Burmese is unmeasured; misrecognition raises anxiety and mislabels learner errors ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391455139_A_comparison_of_the_effects_of_AI-based_chatbots_and_peer_interactions_on_speaking_anxiety_among_EFL_learners)) | Gate phase 2 on an in-house benchmark; use "didn't catch that," never low scores; keep scoring coarse |
| **LLM and TTS teach wrong Burmese** | No model clears 50 on SEA-HELM Burmese; TTS tone errors go undetected ([tracker](https://digitalinasia.com/llm-benchmarks-asian-languages-tour/)) | Constrain with scripts and word lists; native review of all generated curriculum; LLM corrections framed as hints |
| **AI-quality reputation** | Duolingo's AI-first episode coincided with DAU growth falling from 56% to 37% ([Slashdot](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/25/06/28/2036249/)) | Label AI features as practice, keep core curriculum human-verified |
| **Licensing contamination** | SLR80, FLORES+ and Wiktionary are share-alike; Tatoeba audio varies by contributor; myanmar-tools licence listed inconsistently ([Tatoeba](https://tatoeba.org/oc/downloads); [npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/myanmar-tools)) | Keep third-party data in separately licensed asset packs; log the source of every asset; read the myanmar-tools LICENSE |
| **Encoding bugs** | Zawgyi input and diacritic ordering break answer checks ([GALA](https://gala-global.org/knowledge-center/professional-development/articles/zawgyi-vs-unicode-burmese-font-dilemma)) | Detect and convert on input, normalize before comparison, fail CI builds on Zawgyi |
| **Platform cost and security drift** | Storage and Functions need Blaze; App Check is mandatory for AI Logic; API keys must never ship to the browser ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/ai-logic/migrate-from-preview)) | Stay on Spark for phase 1; on Blaze set a budget alert, per-user AI rate limits and secrets in functions only |
| **Early drop-out and streak creep** | Drop-out peaks in the first days; streaks can displace learning ([AERA Open](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858416657002); [Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)) | Tie the streak to clearing due reviews, offer freezes, and measure learning separately from activity |
| **Unverified vendor claims** | Many speech and model figures came from search snippets; Chirp 3 Burmese is in Preview ([Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/speech-to-text/docs/models/chirp-3)) | Re-check live docs and prices before committing to a vendor; keep AI calls behind one swappable service module |
