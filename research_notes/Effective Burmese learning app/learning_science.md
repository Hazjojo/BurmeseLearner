# Learning Science for a Lean Burmese Self-Study App (Adult English-Speaking Beginners)

Evidence-grade legend used below: **STRONG** = multiple meta-analyses, consistent effects; **MODERATE** = a meta-analysis or several good studies, with caveats or moderators; **WEAK** = small or few studies, or mixed results; **NONE/NEGATIVE** = no support, or evidence against.
Method note: research was done through web search snippets plus abstracts. Two hosts were blocked in this session (expertium.github.io, which hosts the FSRS benchmark write-up, and resolve.cambridge.org, which hosts the SSLA 2025 HVPT meta-analysis), so some numbers are taken from secondary summaries and are flagged as such. Allowing those hosts in the session's network settings would let the figures be checked against the originals.

## 1. Retrieval practice / testing effect for L2 vocabulary (incl. recall vs recognition formats)

### Takeaway
**STRONG.** Practice testing beats restudy, with g ≈ 0.50 overall and a larger effect at retention intervals over one day (g ≈ 0.69). Dunlosky et al. rate it one of only two "high utility" techniques. For L2 vocabulary, the retrieval *format* matters. Productive (L1→L2) recall builds the most productive and spelling knowledge. Receptive (L2→L1) recall gives more words learned per minute. Recognition (multiple choice) helps form–meaning mapping but transfers less to production.

### Cited Findings
- Rowland's (2014) meta-analysis puts the testing effect, against restudy, at Hedges' g = 0.50. The advantage grows when the retention interval is more than a day (g = 0.69). — [Springer CRPI 2023 citing Rowland](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s41235-023-00508-8); [PubMed abstract, Rowland 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/)
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), *Review of Educational Research* 87(3), 659–701, is a meta-analysis of practice testing. A later review reports its overall g as about 0.70. I could not confirm the commonly cited overall g = 0.61 or any foreign-language-vocabulary subgroup figure, so treat both as unverified. — [Adesope et al. 2017 PDF (WSU)](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/); [Educ Psych Review 2021 citing it](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) gave "high utility" to only two of 10 techniques, practice testing and distributed practice, because they help learners of different ages and abilities. — [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/26173288/); [AFT summary by Dunlosky](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky); [Kent State summary](https://www.kent.edu/kent/news/success/all-study-strategies-not-created-equal-according-kent-state-researchers)
- Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020, *Modern Language Journal*) meta-analysed intentional vocabulary activities: flashcards, word lists, writing, and fill-in-the-blanks. It used 100 effect sizes from 22 studies, analysed separately for meaning-recall and form-recall tests. Delayed post-test gains ranged from about 23.9% to 73.4% across activities (from a Chinese-language abstract, so verify). Place of study and direction of learning (L1→L2 vs L2→L1) moderated learning. I did not find the flashcard-specific pooled figure. — [x-mol abstract page](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1341861690617110528)
- Nakata (dissertation, plus IRAL 2015) separates four retrieval formats: receptive recall, productive recall, receptive recognition, and productive recognition. Productive recall was especially good for learning orthography (spelling), even though it lowers accuracy during the learning phase. For form–meaning connections, recognition formats did better than recall in his study. — [IRAL 2015, Nakata](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en); [CiNii record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1390001206045723520?lang=en)
- A secondary review summarises Nakata & Webb (2016) and related work: receptive recall is more efficient in words learned per minute, but productive recall gives the biggest overall gains, especially on productive tests. — [Serfaty PhD thesis, Univ. Barcelona (secondary)](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/196300/1/JONATHAN%20SERFATY_PhD_THESIS.pdf)
- A small Japanese study (n = 18) found productive retrieval best on productive tests, while receptive retrieval matched productive retrieval on receptive tests (transfer-appropriate processing). It is tentative given the sample. — [J-STAGE KATE journal 2016](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/katejournal/30/0/30_11/_article)
- An SSLA paper examines within-session repeated retrieval ("Does repeated practice make perfect?"). Its existence is relevant to deciding how many in-session repetitions to schedule, but I did not retrieve its numbers. — [Cambridge SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E)

### Inferences
- The core loop should be *active recall with feedback*, not "see the card, tap next." Default to recall: show Burmese audio or script, learner produces the meaning, then self-grades or types. Add productive recall (English → say or type Burmese) once a card has been learned receptively.
- Multiple choice is a reasonable *first exposure* or scaffold, given Nakata's form–meaning result. It should not be the main review mode, because it trains recognition, which transfers poorly to speaking.
- Typing Burmese script as productive recall also trains orthography (Nakata). For absolute beginners, speaking aloud plus self-grading, or tile assembly of syllables, is a lower-friction productive format until the keyboard is learned.

### Gaps
- Pooled effect for flashcards specifically in Webb et al. (2020), and the Adesope foreign-language subgroup g, could not be retrieved (full texts not accessible).
- No study found on spoken-response retrieval scored by ASR, compared with typed or self-graded recall.

## 2. Spacing and spaced-repetition algorithms (expanding vs fixed; Cepeda; FSRS vs SM-2)

### Takeaway
**STRONG** for spacing itself. An L2-specific meta-analysis (Kim & Webb 2022) finds a medium-to-large benefit. **NONE** for expanding over equal intervals: in L2 studies they perform about the same. The optimal gap grows with the retention interval. **MODERATE (non-peer-reviewed, developer-run)** for FSRS being more accurate than SM-2 at *predicting* recall. Claims about how many reviews FSRS saves come from the FSRS community and were not independently verified.

### Cited Findings
- Kim & Webb (2022), *Language Learning* 72(1), 269–319, pooled 98 effect sizes from 48 experiments (N = 3,411). Findings: spacing gave a medium-to-large L2 benefit; shorter spacing matched longer spacing on immediate tests but fell behind on delayed tests; **equal and expanding schedules performed about the same**. Moderators were learning target, number of sessions, practice type, activity type, feedback timing, and retention interval. One later review calls the effect "small to medium" and cites 37 studies; the abstract says 48. — [BL catalogue record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100152978253_0x000001); [Kim thesis, Western Univ.](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/); [SSLLT 2022 citing it](https://dx.doi.org/10.14746/ssllt.2022.12.3.2)
- Cepeda et al. (2008) tested over 1,350 people with gaps of up to 3.5 months and final tests up to one year later. Performance first rose and then fell as the gap grew, and the optimal gap grew with test delay. As a share of the delay, the optimal gap fell from about 20–40% for a one-week test to about 5–10% for a one-year test. — [PubMed 19076480](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- The FSRS community benchmark (open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark) scores schedulers on log loss, AUC, and a binned RMSE. SM-2 does not output recall probabilities natively, so the benchmark adds formulas to it and also includes a "trainable SM-2" variant. — [srsbench R package README](https://archive.linux.duke.edu/cran/web/packages/srsbench/readme/README.html); [Expertium benchmark page (not fetchable here)](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html)
- A forum claim citing the benchmark says FSRS-5 beats trainable SM-2 in 97.4% of user collections. A blog lists log loss of 0.397 for Leitner, 0.354 for SM-2, and 0.327 for FSRS v4. **Neither was verified against the official table.** — [AnkiWeb forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/fsrs-vs-sm2-in-2-seperate-profiles/52171); [DEV Community blog (unverified)](https://dev.to/palachdigital_20fd679f5fe/why-fsrs-is-better-than-sm-2-2m17)
- A 2026 arXiv paper describes the srs-benchmark as a public leaderboard of memory models. It says the current FSRS-7 adds short-term stability as a third state variable and has 34 weights. — [arXiv 2610.10548](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.10548)
- History and rationale of FSRS, written by community participants. — [LessWrong: the history of FSRS for Anki](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/G7fpGCi8r7nCKXsQk/the-history-of-fsrs-for-anki)

### Inferences
- Any reasonable spaced scheduler captures most of the spacing benefit. Expanding intervals offer no L2 advantage over fixed ones (Kim & Webb), so the scheduler's main value is *efficiency*: fewer wasted reviews at a target retention. That is FSRS's claimed strength.
- For a lean app, using an open-source FSRS implementation (MIT-licensed libraries exist in several languages) costs little and is defensible. A fixed-ladder or SM-2-style scheduler would still capture most of the learning benefit. Grade FSRS's superiority as "credible but self-benchmarked": the benchmark is run by the FSRS developers, though on open data and code.
- Offer a "desired retention" setting of about 0.85–0.90. Lower settings mean fewer reviews and more forgetting. This is a design inference, not something tested in the sources above.

### Gaps
- Exact current srs-benchmark numbers (FSRS-6/7 vs SM-2 log loss, RMSE(bins), dataset size) could not be fetched; expertium.github.io was blocked.
- No peer-reviewed RCT found that compares learning outcomes, rather than prediction accuracy, under FSRS vs SM-2. A KDD 2022 paper by the FSRS authors (Ye et al., MaiMemo SSP-MMC) is the likely peer-reviewed anchor, but it was not retrieved and is not verified here.

## 3. Input: comprehensible input, extensive reading/listening, coverage thresholds, high-frequency vocabulary

### Takeaway
**MODERATE** for extensive reading. Meta-analytic effects are positive and larger for adults (d ≈ 0.70 in the 2015 version), but selection bias likely inflates them (an adjusted estimate is about 0.37). **STRONG** for the coverage principle: unassisted comprehension needs about 95% (minimum) to 98% (optimal) known running words. **WEAK/UNVERIFIED** for Krashen's strong claim that input *alone* suffices: no meta-analytic support was found in this search. Coverage figures are for English and may not transfer to Burmese.

### Cited Findings
- Jeon & Day (2016), *Reading in a Foreign Language* 28(2), pooled 71 samples from 49 studies (1980–2014; N = 5,919). Adults showed larger effects than children or adolescents. EFL settings beat ESL settings, and web-based stories beat paper books. In the 2015 working version, adults had d = 0.70, children 0.52, and adolescents 0.35. — [NFLRC RFL item](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/354); [ScholarSpace](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/b52a5591-ec04-4026-816f-434361a4e899)
- Hamada (2020) argues that earlier syntheses overestimated extensive reading's effect through selection bias. Using propensity-score methods in one Japanese university setting, it estimates about 0.37 for reading comprehension. — [Frontiers / PMC7188915](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188915)
- Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) propose an *optimal* threshold of about 8,000 word families for 98% coverage and a *minimal* threshold of 4,000–5,000 families for 95% coverage, proper nouns included, for reading. — [RFL 22(1) PDF](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/66648/1/22_1_10125_66648_laufer.pdf)
- Nation (2006) found 6,000–7,000 families needed for 98% coverage of spoken English and 8,000–9,000 for written. A replication (Schmitt, Cobb, Horst et al.) re-examines this. Schmitt et al. (2011) proposed 98% as the appropriate target and 95% as the minimum. — [Language Teaching replication](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)
- How these thresholds should be measured (word families vs lemmas vs flemmas; "mastery" thresholds) is disputed between Laufer (2021) and McLean & Stoeckel. — [Reply to Laufer, RFL 33(2)](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/67403/1/33_2_10125-67403.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a beginner app, the practical implication is to **deliberately pre-teach high-frequency vocabulary** with SRS, then provide **narrow, highly controlled input**: graded stories or dialogues in which nearly all words are already known (98% coverage). Burmese graded readers are scarce, so the app likely has to author or LLM-generate leveled texts restricted to the learner's known-word list.
- "Pure CI" (input only, no deliberate study) is a poor fit for a distant language with a new script and no shared cognates. Beginners cannot reach 95% coverage of authentic Burmese for a long time.
- Burmese is analytic, with heavy particle use and common compounding. Word-family counts from English do not map directly, so frequency lists and coverage should be computed on a segmented Burmese corpus. Burmese script has no spaces between words, so segmentation is non-trivial.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis on extensive *listening* or narrow listening was retrieved.
- No empirical critique or meta-analysis of Krashen's Input Hypothesis came up. Known critiques, such as McLaughlin 1987 and Swain's immersion findings, are not verified here.
- No coverage or frequency studies for Burmese were found.

## 4. Output, interaction, and corrective feedback (Swain, Long; Lyster & Saito; Li 2010)

### Takeaway
**STRONG/MODERATE.** Corrective feedback has a medium overall effect that lasts over time. Prompts (making the learner self-correct) beat recasts (simply giving the correct form) in classroom studies. Lab studies show larger effects than classroom studies. The research is about teacher–learner interaction, so app-delivered automated feedback is an extrapolation.

### Cited Findings
- Lyster & Saito (2010, SSLA) is a meta-analysis of 15 classroom studies (N = 827). Recasts had medium effects and prompts had large effects, and prompts were significantly more effective than recasts in within-group contrasts. Younger learners benefited more than older ones. — [Cambridge SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1); [McGill eScholarship](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)
- Mackey & Goo (2007), as reported by Lyster & Saito, found interaction effects of d = 0.57 on immediate posttests and d = 0.76 on long-delayed classroom posttests. — [Cambridge SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)
- Li (2010, *Language Learning* 60(2), 309–365) included both lab and classroom studies. It found a medium overall corrective-feedback effect that held over time. Implicit feedback was better maintained than explicit feedback, and lab studies showed larger effects than classroom ones. The exact pooled d was not retrieved; d ≈ 0.61 is often quoted but unverified here. — [Springer AJSFL 2020 review](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s40862-020-00097-9.pdf); [Language Teaching review of oral CF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/abs/oral-corrective-feedback-in-second-language-classrooms/B33FC71C12A1317DCA0CE704B7B0BC00)

### Inferences
- In-app: when a learner types or speaks a wrong answer, **prompt first** (for example, "check the tone," or highlight the wrong syllable) and only then reveal the answer. This mirrors the prompt-over-recast finding, and it also adds a retrieval attempt.
- Speaking practice (output) gives "pushed output," a forced chance to notice gaps. A lean version is record-and-compare: the learner speaks, then hears the native model and their own recording side by side. Automated scoring of Burmese tones is technically hard (see section 7).

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis on *automated* or ASR-based CF for speaking was retrieved. AI conversation partners (LLM chat) lack meta-analytic evidence as of this search.

## 5. Formulaic language / chunks / sentence-based learning; sentence mining

### Takeaway
**MODERATE.** Instruction on collocations has a large pooled effect (d ≈ 1.4), but it rests on 17 mostly small studies. Multiword sequences show a processing advantage. No meta-analysis directly compares "sentence cards vs isolated-word cards." Sentence mining is a community practice without controlled trials.

### Cited Findings
- A meta-analysis in IRAL (doi 10.1515/iral-2021-0218) of 17 studies (N = 3,859) on instruction for L2 collocation learning found a large overall effect: d = 1.415, 95% CI [1.189, 1.641]. — [De Gruyter IRAL](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2021-0218/html)
- A 2023 Cambridge meta-analysis on *processing* of multiword sequences found a processing advantage for most subtypes, with small-to-medium effects. — [Cambridge core reader](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/product/64D9BFF2B458422C8CCE202A520F914A/core-reader)
- Studies of deliberately learned L2 multiword items show that frequency affects form recall. — [Benjamins ITL](https://benjamins.com/catalog/itl.18005.lin)

### Inferences
- For Burmese, teach high-frequency **chunks as units** from day one: greetings, polite sentence-final particles, question frames, and verb + particle patterns. Burmese grammar lives largely in particles, so chunk cards carry grammar implicitly.
- Hybrid design: SRS cards for the *word*, always shown with an audio example sentence, plus separate cards for very frequent phrases. This is an inference, not directly tested.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found comparing isolated-word vs sentence-context flashcards on retention. Sentence mining (i+1 sentences from media) is anecdotal and community-driven, with no peer-reviewed trial found.

## 6. Explicit vs implicit grammar instruction

### Takeaway
**STRONG/MODERATE.** Explicit instruction (rule explanation and/or metalinguistic feedback) beats implicit instruction in two meta-analyses (Norris & Ortega 2000: d ≈ 1.13 explicit vs 0.54 implicit; Spada & Tomita 2010: explicit > implicit for both simple and complex features). A major caveat is outcome measures: few studies used free production, so the advantage may partly reflect test format.

### Cited Findings
- Norris & Ortega (2000), with 49 unique samples: focused L2 instruction gives large target-oriented gains, and explicit types beat implicit ones. A secondary source gives d = 1.13 for 69 explicit treatments and d = 0.54 for 29 implicit ones. — [Columbia SALT "Another look at Norris & Ortega"](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1434); [DeepDyve abstract](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/wiley/does-type-of-instruction-make-a-difference-substantive-findings-from-a-9dzVAhpcHv/)
- Critiques: a reexamination identifies weaknesses in data collection, coding, and statistics. Only about 16% of the studies used free constructed-response measures. — [Columbia SALT](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1434); [DOAJ SALT 2010](https://doaj.org/article/01b7af7b486a479da9c615ae8cb7c9a7)
- Spada & Tomita (2010), with 41 studies coded explicit vs implicit following Norris & Ortega, found larger effect sizes for explicit instruction for both simple and complex features. Exact effect sizes were not retrieved. — [Univ. Toronto EAS listing](https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790)

### Inferences
- A lean app should include **short explicit grammar notes**: one-screen explanations of each particle or construction, with examples and audio, introduced just before the learner meets the pattern in practice. Avoid long grammar lectures. The evidence supports brief, focused explicitness followed by practice.

### Gaps
- No evidence found specific to self-study apps, or to Burmese's particle-heavy grammar.

## 7. Pronunciation and lexical tone training (HVPT, visual pitch feedback, shadowing) — with Burmese specifics

### Takeaway
**STRONG/MODERATE** for perceptual phonetic training. HVPT improves L2 perception, transfers to production with small-to-medium effects, and the classic Mandarin tone study showed about 21 percentage points of gain after eight sessions, retained at six months. **WEAK/contested** for "talker variability" being the active ingredient: one meta-analysis found the effect vanished after bias correction. **WEAK/MIXED** for visual pitch-contour feedback. **No evidence found** on shadowing. **Burmese warning:** Burmese tones are cued by phonation (creaky, glottal), duration, and vowel quality as well as pitch, so pitch-only tools such as F0 contour displays capture only part of the contrast.

### Cited Findings
- Wang, Spence, Jongman & Sereno (1999, *JASA* 106, 3649–3658): eight American learners of Mandarin received eight high-variability training sessions over two weeks. Tone identification rose from about 69% to 90% (+21 points), generalized to new stimuli (+18%) and new talkers (+25%), and was retained at six months. These figures are from secondary citations. — [KU preprint](https://kuppl.ku.edu/sites/kuppl/files/documents/publications/wang_etal_revision.pdf); [KU ScholarWorks](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/499786a0-dec8-4422-aaf9-68909b608369)
- Wang, Jongman & Sereno (2002): perceptual tone gains transferred to production, with trainees' tones judged more accurate by native listeners. — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/213404728.pdf)
- Uchihara, Karas & Thomson: a meta-analysis of 31 studies on whether *perceptual* HVPT improves *production* found small-to-medium improvements (43 within-participant and 17 between-participant effect sizes). — [Applied Psycholinguistics 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38D8F5CE65DC708137B0E95F97C6BC7)
- An SSLA 2025 HVPT meta-analysis of 79 perceptual training studies examined moderators including total training time, number of talkers, and target phones. The overall g and the tone-specific subgroup were not retrievable (host blocked). — [Cambridge SSLA core reader](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6/core-reader)
- A talker-variability meta-analysis (JSLHR 2021, 18 studies) found g = 0.46 [0.08, 0.84] for immediate outcomes, which **dropped to g = −0.04 after removing outliers and correcting for publication bias**. — [JSLHR 2021](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_JSLHR-21-00181)
- A general L2 phonetic training meta-analysis (JSLHR 2025) found a large effect, d = 0.762, with perceptual training larger than production training. — [JSLHR 2024/25](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/2024_JSLHR-24-00432)
- Visual pitch feedback is mixed. A BU study (n = 16) found **no improvement** in tone production, and no extra benefit from visual feedback. Zhou & Olson (2023) found production gains for isolated words, varying by tone. Another study found perception and production gains linked to the F0 *slope* (contour) cue. — [BU OpenBU](https://open.bu.edu/items/abd739eb-7dd7-420d-bb61-48d7e7e8e003); [MDPI Languages 8(3):209](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-471X/8/3/209); [PMC12293670](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12293670)
- **Burmese specifics:** Burmese has a four-way contrast (low, high, creaky, glottal/checked) cued by pitch, contour, duration, *and* phonation. Mandarin tones are mainly pitch-cued. — [PubMed 30343621, Tsukada & Kondo 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30343621); [Macquarie record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/the-perception-of-mandarin-lexical-tones-by-native-speakers-of-bu/)
- In an acoustic analysis of Burmese, creaky and stopped tones were differentiated mainly by vowel quality, and analyses of the cues conflict. — [UT Austin repository](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/19915)
- Lai argues that F0 is a more primary cue than phonation, based on neutralization data. — [ICU Working Papers, Lai](https://icu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/5250/files/ICUWPL18-11_Lai.pdf)
- Psychoacoustic roughness identified creakiness in Burmese creaky and checked tones more reliably (Japanese KAKEN project). — [KAKEN 15K16745](https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/grant/KAKENHI-PROJECT-15K16745)
- Burmese is often described as a register language, where tone and phonation co-vary. — [Wikipedia: Register (phonology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_(phonology))

### Inferences
- **Build:** a minimal-pair tone perception trainer using HVPT. Use multiple native speakers, with identification tasks (hear a syllable, pick the tone or the meaning) and immediate feedback. This is a cheap feature with the strongest evidence among pronunciation tools. Recording 4–6 speakers (male and female) is worth the content cost even though the talker-variability effect is debated: multiple talkers do no harm and may help generalization.
- **Consider carefully:** a pitch-contour visualizer. Evidence is mixed, and for Burmese it would miss creak and glottal closure, so it could mislead learners on two of the four tones. If built, show waveform or duration and voice-quality cues too, or present it as "compare your recording with the native one."
- Build perception training before production. Transfer from perception to production is documented, with small-to-medium effects.

### Gaps
- No studies found on English speakers *learning Burmese tones*. The evidence is extrapolated from Mandarin, Thai, Cantonese, and general HVPT.
- No meta-analytic or controlled evidence on shadowing was retrieved.
- Tone-specific pooled HVPT effect sizes were not retrievable.

## 8. Learning a new script (Burmese abugida)

### Takeaway
**NO DIRECT EVIDENCE RETRIEVED.** This search found no meta-analyses or controlled studies on adult acquisition of a new script, or of Burmese script specifically. Recommendations here rest on inference from the general principles above: retrieval, spacing, mnemonics with pictures, and dual coding.

### Cited Findings
- Keyword or picture mnemonics help long-term retention when *pictures are provided*. Self-generated keywords did not reduce forgetting (Thomas & Wang 1996). Relevant to letter-shape mnemonics by analogy. — [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib1990/601)
- Productive recall (writing or typing the form) especially helps learning orthography (Nakata). — [IRAL 2015](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)

### Inferences
- Introduce the script incrementally, starting with high-frequency consonants plus the inherent vowel and common vowel diacritics. Always pair glyphs with audio ("reading alongside audio"). Give each consonant a picture mnemonic that the app *provides* rather than asking the learner to invent one. Use spaced retrieval with audio → glyph and glyph → sound cards. Offer romanization only as a temporary scaffold that fades out. These are all inferences, not tested for Burmese.
- Burmese writes tone partly through diacritics (for example, the dot below for creaky tone and the visarga for high tone). Teaching tone marks *together with* the tone perception trainer would link visual and auditory codes. This is a dual-coding inference.

### Gaps
- No studies retrieved on optimal script-introduction order, "read-along" (synchronized text + audio) effects, or transliteration scaffolding for adult learners of an abugida. This is a priority follow-up search, for example on Hindi/Devanagari, Thai, or Korean Hangul learning studies.

## 9. Interleaving, desirable difficulties, generation, dual coding, keyword mnemonic

### Takeaway
Keyword mnemonic: **WEAK/MIXED**. Dunlosky rated it low utility. It helps immediate recall, but later forgetting can be faster unless pictures are provided or it is combined with retrieval practice. Interleaving, generation, and dual coding for L2 vocabulary: **not verified in this search**, so no graded claims are made.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated five techniques low utility: summarization, highlighting, the **keyword mnemonic**, imagery for text learning, and rereading. — [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/26173288/); [UNB summary](https://www.unb.ca/fredericton/cetl/services/teaching-tips/instructional-methods/effective-learning-techniques.html)
- Wang, Thomas & Ouellette (1992): long-term forgetting was greater with the keyword mnemonic than with rote rehearsal. Thomas & Wang (1996): providing pictures of the keyword and referent improved long-term retention. — [UCF STARS 1992](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus1990/1045); [UCF STARS 1996](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib1990/601)
- A 2024 Heliyon study (n = 110 Chinese university students) found keyword mnemonics combined with retrieval practice performed well compared with restudy and retrieval alone. The full comparison was not verified. — [PMC10839596](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10839596)
- Some EFL classroom studies report keyword groups outperforming memorization groups in learning and retention. These are smaller, lower-quality studies. — [DOAJ JELTL 2013](https://doaj.org/article/a54e7d8e3fc3478f866d48ef58586f55)
- Chiu (2013) meta-analysed computer-assisted vocabulary learning: d = 0.75, a moderate effect. — [ReCALL meta-analysis, Yu & Trainin (citing Chiu)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/08A549A6CFD1078406E6A4F8AFE28184/core-reader)

### Inferences
- Keyword mnemonics are a weaker fit for Burmese than for European languages because English sound-alikes are harder to find. If offered, they should be optional, curated, illustrated, and always followed by retrieval practice.
- An SRS that mixes new and old items already interleaves naturally, so no special feature is needed.

### Gaps
- No meta-analytic data retrieved for interleaving in L2 vocabulary or grammar, the generation effect in L2, or dual coding / picture glosses in L2 vocabulary. A known SSLA meta-analysis on glossing forms exists ("How do different forms of glossing contribute to L2 vocabulary learning from reading?"), but its effect sizes were not retrieved. — [Cambridge SSLA glossing meta-analysis](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)

## 10. Motivation, habit, streaks/gamification, session length, and app drop-out

### Takeaway
**MODERATE** for self-determination theory (SDT): autonomous motivation predicts persistence in app-based study. **WEAK and vendor-sourced** for streaks. Duolingo's own A/B data shows streak features raise retention by small amounts, but independent commentary documents "streak creep": protecting the streak replaces real learning, and broken streaks trigger quitting. Drop-out is heaviest in the first days. Independent, peer-reviewed language-app drop-out rates are scarce.

### Cited Findings
- **Vendor data (Duolingo blog, not peer-reviewed):** learners who reach a 7-day streak are 2.4× more likely to return the next day and 3.6× more likely to finish their course. This is likely confounded by selection: motivated users build streaks. Separating the daily goal from the streak raised Day-14 retention by 3.3%. Allowing up to two Streak Freezes raised daily active learners by +0.38%. — [Duolingo blog: how the streak builds habit](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit); [Duolingo blog: improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- The Decision Lab, citing a *Journal of Consumer Research* study: users come to value extending the streak over the underlying activity, users who break a streak are more likely to quit entirely, and users are less motivated when they know a streak can be repaired. — [The Decision Lab: Streak Creep](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)
- Hwang (2026, *Language Learning & Technology*), 305 Korean adolescents using a commercial app: more autonomous motivation (SDT) predicted returning after shorter gaps and a longer overall usage span. The authors recommend early onboarding and motivational support. — [LLT journal](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1273/)
- A 2024 SSLA study of 3,670 adult mobile-language-learning users (survival analysis): more frequent pauses combined with longer active use and shorter breaks predicted fewer drop-outs. — [Cambridge SSLA 2024 PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C1186B329F808A11DDF2C748E77FD1EE/S0272263124000354a.pdf/div-class-title-acceptance-and-engagement-patterns-of-mobile-assisted-language-learning-among-non-conventional-adult-l2-learners-a-survival-analysis-div.pdf)
- A 2025 MSU dissertation (Loewen lab) cites Loewen et al. (2020) for a link between in-app activity and proficiency gains, and names high attrition as a major obstacle to app-based self-study. In a 13-week SDT study (n = 258), teacher autonomy support indirectly raised app use. — [MSU dissertation](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/52279/FULL_TEXT/view)
- MOOC drop-out is heaviest in the first days and levels off after about week two. — [AERA Open 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858416657002)
- SDT is the dominant theory used to explain MOOC completion. — [WUR review 2022](https://edepot.wur.nl/565892)
- Language-MOOC persistence (n = 197): learner interaction and alignment of content with the learner's goals predicted higher persistence and lower boredom. — [Computers in Human Behavior Reports 2025 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/975130355ba848719ef3df09ba85ceac)

### Inferences
- Put the most retention-design effort into the **first 1–2 weeks**: fast first win, learner sets their own goal (autonomy), visible competence such as "you can now read X syllables" or "you understood this dialogue." That fits the SDT findings and the early-drop-out pattern.
- If a streak is included, make it **gentle**. Tie it to a meaningful minimum (for example, due reviews cleared) rather than one trivial tap, allow freezes, and don't make breaking it feel catastrophic. Avoid leaderboards and gem economies, which have no learning evidence here.
- Short daily sessions fit both the spacing evidence and the survival-analysis finding that regular sessions with short breaks predict persistence.

### Gaps
- No independent, peer-reviewed drop-out or churn rate for Duolingo or other language apps was found. The widely repeated "~90%+ quit" figures could not be sourced.
- No controlled study comparing session length (for example, 5 vs 20 minutes per day) on L2 outcomes was retrieved.

## 11. Common ineffective practices and myths

### Takeaway
**Evidence against** learning-styles matching (no support for the "meshing" hypothesis). Rereading and highlighting are **low utility**. Recognition-heavy, gamified tapping is likely inefficient for production by transfer-appropriate-processing logic, but there is no direct meta-analysis.

### Cited Findings
- Pashler et al. (2008) found virtually no evidence for the interaction pattern needed to justify learning-styles assessments; very few studies even used an adequate design, and several contradicted meshing. — [PubMed 26162104](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104/)
- A 2015 follow-up that used Pashler's design found no support for meshing in adults. A 2024 meta-analysis covers 21 matched-vs-unmatched instruction studies, but its findings were not retrieved. — [Frontiers 2018 / PMC6113575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6113575/); [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1428732/full)
- Rereading and highlighting are low utility. Students rely on them heavily, but they do not consistently boost performance. — [AFT / Dunlosky](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky)
- Recognition practice transfers less to productive knowledge than productive recall does. — [IRAL 2015, Nakata](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en); [J-STAGE 2016](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/katejournal/30/0/30_11/_article)
- A product case study argues that streak protection leads users to do the shortest possible lesson. This is analytical commentary, not data. — [Case study portfolio](https://jiwonvyoo.github.io/portfolio/case/duolingo/duo.html)

### Inferences
- Leave out: learning-style quizzes and "visual/auditory learner" modes, passive "review list" screens with no retrieval, and tap-the-matching-pair games as the main practice mode.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of gamified recognition tasks vs recall tasks inside commercial apps was found.

## 12. Synthesis for feature decisions (build / maybe / skip)

### Takeaway
A lean Burmese app with good evidence behind it would rest on four things: (1) spaced **retrieval** (recall-first, productive later) on high-frequency words and chunks; (2) an **HVPT tone/phonation perception trainer** with multiple native talkers; (3) **brief explicit grammar notes** on particles; and (4) **controlled, high-coverage audio + script input** (graded micro-stories). Gentle, autonomy-supportive habit features complete it. Gamified recognition, learning styles, and heavy streak economies have weak or no evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Practice testing and distributed practice are the only two high-utility techniques. — [Dunlosky 2013, PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/26173288/)
- L2 spacing gives a medium-to-large benefit, and expanding ≈ equal schedules. — [Kim & Webb 2022 record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100152978253_0x000001)
- Perceptual HVPT transfers to production with small-to-medium effects. — [Applied Psycholinguistics 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38D8F5CE65DC708137B0E95F97C6BC7)
- Explicit > implicit grammar instruction. — [Columbia SALT](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1434); [Spada & Tomita listing](https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790)
- 98% coverage is the optimal target for unassisted comprehension. — [Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/66648/1/22_1_10125_66648_laufer.pdf)
- Prompts > recasts for corrective feedback. — [Lyster & Saito 2010](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)

### Inferences
- **BUILD (strong evidence):** an SRS with recall cards (FSRS via an open-source library, or a simple fixed ladder); audio on every card; productive recall stage; tone minimal-pair HVPT trainer; short grammar notes; prompt-then-reveal feedback.
- **BUILD (moderate evidence):** chunk/phrase cards; graded stories restricted to known words (98% target); record-and-compare speaking; gentle streak with freezes; early-week onboarding focused on competence and autonomy.
- **MAYBE (weak/mixed):** pitch-contour visualization (pitch-only, so incomplete for Burmese creaky/glottal tones); keyword mnemonics (curated, illustrated, optional); LLM conversation partner (no meta-analytic evidence found).
- **SKIP (none/negative):** learning-style personalization; matching-pair or recognition-only games as the core loop; leaderboards, gem economies, and punitive streak loss; passive reread or word-list screens.

### Gaps
- Direct evidence on Burmese L2 acquisition (tones, script, vocabulary frequency) is essentially absent from the literature retrieved here. Most Burmese-specific design choices are extrapolations from Mandarin/Thai tone research and English-based vocabulary research.
